# RAISE Price Increase Letter (Raising Prices on Existing Customers)
slug: raise-price-letter · category: pricing · aliases: price raise playbook, RAISE letter, price increase letter, don't grandfather, stair-step discount

## What it is
A five-part letter for raising prices on current customers without mass cancellations, plus his rules for doing it. His friend Trey, losing money on $49/mo gym memberships, tripled prices using an earlier version and went from losing about $2,000 a month to making about $4,000 within a week.

## How to apply it
The letter (R-A-I-S-E):
- R, Remind them of value already delivered, personalized with their usage and results.
- A, Address the change directly in one sentence.
- I, Invest in their future: list the improvements you already planned, each tied to more of what they like or less of what they hate.
- S, Soften it with a loyalty reward: keep them on the old rate for 3-6 months as a credit, then move them up.
- E, Explain away concerns: invite personal replies, with a PS offering to work something out if the increase would materially hurt them.

Rules around it:
1. Test the new price on new customers first.
2. Do not grandfather or sell lifetime deals.
3. Raise at least yearly ([[annual-price-increases]]).
4. Call customers personally if the jump is over 50%.
5. Time it within 90 days of a launch.
6. Know how many customers you can lose and still come out ahead.
7. Move new customers to the new price immediately.
8. For big jumps, stair-step the discount away in two or three drops.
9. In a community, post a video with comments off; route questions to you.

## When to reach for it
- Prices far below value, or a business bleeding cash.

## Mistakes he warns about
- Promising investments you won't make, or adding new expenses that eat the extra profit.
- Resenting early adopters instead of letting price rise with the product.

## The math
Expect three groups: those who see the value and stay, those truly hurt (extend their discount), and those who were leaving anyway. Churn usually spikes the first month, dips below normal the next, then returns to baseline, meaning cancellations were pulled forward. A software company moved its largest accounts from $30 to about $3,000/mo by phone and lost one of 300.
