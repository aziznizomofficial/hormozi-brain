# Brand Bouquet (Market Moves, Pivots and Recovering from Mistakes)
slug: brand-bouquet · category: marketing · aliases: bouquet metaphor, up/down/adjacent/broader/narrower market, brand pivots, net audience change, dead rose, overwhelm the bad pairing

## What it is
Hormozi's picture of a brand as a bouquet. Individual products, values, people and experiences are single flowers. The brand is what they form when bundled together. What you add, from where and how much decides what the bouquet looks like. The metaphor explains how to steer a brand into new markets on purpose, how to judge a pivot (did you gain more audience than you lost?), and how to recover when a bad pairing gets in.

## How to apply it
1. **Pick a direction** to grow your addressable market, using his assisted-living law firm as the example:
   - **Up-market:** bigger customers, such as multi-location owners.
   - **Down-market:** smaller ones, such as at-home or mobile care.
   - **Adjacent:** a similar but different market, such as rehab facilities.
   - **Broader:** the whole category, such as all senior living.
   - **Narrower:** a slice, such as facilities with 100–500 residents.
2. **Change the flowers to match.** Talk about, make products for and appear alongside what the new target likes. Narrowing is like going from mixed roses to red roses only. An adjacent move is like swapping red roses for yellow.
3. **Watch the audience's reaction** and treat every change as a bet. Only make moves where you expect a net gain, like a local band that hits the big time: losing a few original fans is fine if far more new ones arrive.
4. **When a bad pairing happens** (a scandal, a DUI, a bad partner), do not try to erase it. Flood the bouquet with more of what your core audience likes until the bad flower shrinks into irrelevance.

## When to reach for it
- Planning to expand into a new segment, or narrow to a niche.
- Content topics have drifted and the "wrong" views are growing.
- Handling a public mistake or controversy.

## Mistakes he warns about
- Adding "sticks and weeds," pairings that do not fit and muddy the whole brand.
- Changing direction without checking the net effect. Pepsi's protest ad lost more people than it gained.
- Letting a handful of critics stop a move that wins far more new fans.

## The math
Every brand move is either a net gain or a net loss in audience. One-for-one swaps only exist on paper. His ratio: ignore five mean comments if the change wins 500 new fans.
