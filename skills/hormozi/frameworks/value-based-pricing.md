# Value-Based Pricing (and Price Testing)
slug: value-based-pricing · category: pricing · aliases: three pricing models, cost-plus vs competitor-based vs value-based, willingness to pay, price testing table, finding the perfect price

## What it is
There are three ways to set a price. Cost-plus adds a margin to your costs; it is simple, but customers neither know nor care about your costs, and you leave money on the table with everyone who would pay more. Competitor-based copies the market average; it is simple but built on someone else's business and customers. Value-based pricing sets price by what the customer is willing to pay, which rises as you make the product more valuable. It forces you to keep improving the product and talking to customers.

## How to apply it
1. Pick the goal: the best price is the one that makes the most money over the customer's lifetime, not the one that wins the most customers or the biggest first purchase.
2. Track two metrics per price: conversion rate (how many buy) and churn (how long they stay or how often they rebuy).
3. Build a simple table: price, traffic, conversion, sales, churn, lifetime value, total return from the same traffic.
4. Test new prices on new customers first; if conversion and churn hold up, roll the price to the existing base (see [[raise-price-letter]]).
5. Keep testing between your best two data points.

## When to reach for it
- You set prices by looking at competitors or marking up costs.
- You have never tested a price.

## Mistakes he warns about
- Assuming a bigger price jump always yields more; past a point the conversion and churn losses outweigh the gain.
- Judging a price on first-purchase revenue instead of lifetime gross profit.
- Going back to the old price at the first "no".

## The math
Per 100 clicks:
- $10: 5% convert, 5 sales, 10% churn, LTV $100, total $500.
- $20: 4% convert, 4 sales, 10% churn, LTV $200, total $800 (+60%). A 100% price rise cost only 20% of conversions.
- $100: 2% convert, 2 sales, 33% churn, LTV $300, total $600 (+20%): better than $10, worse than $20.
Next test would sit around $39-59. Rule of thumb: if doubling price cuts closes by only about 20% with everything else equal, do it.
