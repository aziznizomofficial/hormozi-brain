# Virtuous Cycle of Price (Premium Pricing)
slug: virtuous-cycle-of-price · category: pricing · aliases: vicious cycle of price, charge what it's worth, charge so much it hurts, price-to-value discrepancy, premium price leader

## What it is
People buy when what they get feels worth more than what they pay. You can widen that gap by cutting price, but that is a one-way road to zero; he prefers raising value first and then price. Higher prices set off a self-reinforcing loop: buyers commit harder, perceive more value, get better results, behave better, and fund a better product. Lower prices run the loop in reverse.

## How to apply it
1. Stop pricing by copying the market average and shading below it; the competitors you copy are broke.
2. Build a large value gap (see [[value-equation]]) so the buyer still gets a steal at a high price.
3. Price far above competitors, far enough that prospects assume something different is going on (category of one).
4. Reinvest the margin in people, systems, service and marketing so the product earns the price.
5. Price so it stings a little; invested clients follow through.

## When to reach for it
- Demanding, low-paying clients and a service you cannot afford to make great.
- A race to the bottom where every competitor offers a bit more for a bit less.
- You hesitate to charge because of your own self-doubt.

## Mistakes he warns about
- Cutting prices to win customers; ranking second-lowest on price provides no strategic advantage.
- Charging a premium without the delivery to back it; conviction must come from repeated results.
- Assuming cheap clients are grateful; his friend Trey's $49/mo gym attracted the worst customers and lost money until he tripled prices.

## The math
Gym Launch entered a market with $500/mo competitors and one $5,000 player, charging $16,000 for a 16-week program (about 3x the highest and 32x the lowest), then moved about 35% into a $42,000/yr tier; members averaged about +$239,000/yr in revenue. A photography client raised average ticket from $300 to $1,500 and profit went from about $1,000/wk to $38,000/wk. Identical wine rated better when priced higher. His investment principle: customers who spend the largest amounts devote the greatest attention. (p. 71)
