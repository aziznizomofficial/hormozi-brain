# Four-Week Billing & Payment Hacks
slug: four-week-billing · category: pricing · aliases: bill every 4 weeks, 13 billing cycles, 3% processing fee, second form of payment, ACH, paycheck-aligned billing

## What it is
A group of small wording and billing changes that add revenue with no extra delivery work. Hormozi calls four-week billing the highest value-per-word note in Money Models. A year has 13 four-week cycles but only 12 months, and people buy just as readily at "$X every four weeks" as at "$X a month." The companion tactics recover revenue that recurring businesses otherwise lose to failed or expired payments.

## How to apply it
1. **Bill on a weekly multiple** (every week, 2, 4 or 12 weeks) instead of monthly.
2. **Add a processing fee** of about 3% to the quoted price. He says no one has ever refused to buy over it.
3. **Trade the fee for a second payment method.** Offer to waive the 3% if they give a backup method, explaining that chasing failed cards costs you staff time.
4. **Get ACH** (direct bank debit) as the backup where possible. It's the cheapest way to take payment after cash.
5. **Line up charges with paychecks** and, if a charge declines, retry it several times that day, since deposits land at different hours.
6. Check local rules on surcharges before adding fees.

## When to reach for it
Any recurring-revenue business, especially one with thin margins, frequent card declines, or "involuntary" churn from customers whose cards fail even though they didn't mean to cancel.

## Mistakes he warns about
- Assuming billing mechanics are trivial. Payment processing problems quietly cost recurring businesses a lot of cash.
- Running a card once and giving up. Retrying on payday recovers a meaningful share.

## The math
- 13 cycles vs 12 months = about 8.3% more annual revenue for the same work. At a 20% margin that raises annual profit by roughly 41% (p. 157).
- A 3% fee on a 10%-margin business adds about 30% to profit (p. 157).
- Paycheck-timed retries recover about a third of declined payments (p. 118).
