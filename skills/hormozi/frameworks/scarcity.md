# Scarcity (and the Delicate Dance of Desire)
slug: scarcity · category: offer · aliases: FOMO, limited supply, capacity caps, honest scarcity, delay the ask, longer runway bigger plane, fractal demand

## What it is
Marketing moves the supply and demand curve: persuasion raises demand, and limiting supply (real or perceived) raises the price you can charge. Desire exists only while it is unsatisfied, so selling to everyone who wants you drains future demand, while leaving some wanting keeps it building. Scarcity is the quantity-based lever; fear of losing out moves people more than hope of gaining.

## How to apply it
Three forms: limited seats or slots, limited bonuses, and never-available-again.
- Physical products: limited drops (flavors, colors, designs), roughly monthly; always sell out and announce that you did.
- Services: cap the whole business at X clients (with a waitlist), cap growth at X new clients per week, or cap each cohort. Open 10-20% more capacity occasionally, always leaving demand unmet.
- Honest scarcity: decide your real capacity and tell people how full you are.
- Extreme scarcity: a tiny, very expensive tier of direct access to you, in whatever channel you tolerate.
- Small groups: once members leave, they cannot return.
- Even free lead magnets can be rationed per week to raise desire and consumption.

## When to reach for it
- Prospects delay because they believe you will always be available.
- You sell out at a price that is too low.
- You run launches, workshops, events or high-ticket upsells.

## Mistakes he warns about
- Fake limits; they cost credibility.
- Over-ordering and failing to sell out, which breaks the effect.
- Satisfying all demand at a low price; each repeat promotion then sells fewer units.
- Letting desire go unmet forever; people eventually feel rejected.

## The math
Demand is fractal (roughly 80/20): about a fifth of prospects will pay five times more. Ten seats at $500 = $5,000; two one-on-one spots at $5,000 = $10,000, with lower cost and eight people left hungry. A charity he attended cut tickets and raised them from $15,000 to $25,000. His analogy: a greater length of runway allows a larger aircraft to take off. (p. 139)
