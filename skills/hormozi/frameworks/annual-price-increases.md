# Annual Price Increases
slug: annual-price-increases · category: pricing · aliases: CPI increase clause, raise prices every year, See's Candies rule, built-in escalator

## What it is
Costs rise every year, so a price that never moves is a price that falls. He writes a fixed yearly increase into customer contracts, the way landlords raise rent, and treats raising prices at least once a year as a standing rule. His model is Warren Buffett, who let See's Candies managers run everything except price, raising it every year for decades.

## How to apply it
1. Pick a yearly increase, usually 5-15%; few customers object below about 15%.
2. Add it to new contracts and customer agreements.
3. Explain it as keeping pace with the cost of living so you never have to cut quality. Bring it up during paperwork, not during the pitch.
4. Separately, review pricing at least annually: first to cover inflation, then to cover other costs that rose.
5. For larger, one-off increases on existing customers, use [[raise-price-letter]].

## When to reach for it
- Prices have not changed in years.
- Margins are shrinking even though volume is flat or up.
- Recurring or contract-based revenue.

## Mistakes he warns about
- Believing a few percent a year makes no difference; not raising has already cost you, just in the wrong direction.
- Only raising when forced; you lose the benefit of choosing the timing.

## The math
At 5% a year, $100 becomes about $105, $110, $116, $122 over four years (+22%). At 12% a year it reaches about $157. Using public inflation data, $100 in 2024 equals about $79 in 2017, so a business that kept 2017 prices lost about 21% of purchasing power; one with 20% margins in 2017 would have roughly no profit seven years later at the same volume. Buffett raised See's prices about 10% a year on average, up to 17% in some years, over 50 times since buying it.
