# Get Flow, Monetize Flow, Add Friction
slug: flow-monetize-friction · category: money-model · aliases: promotional wrappers, attract offers, free & discount promotions, generate demand first, create flow monetize flow add friction, sales to fulfillment continuum, over-deliver then optimize

## What it is
Hormozi's order of operations for entering any new market: first generate lead flow, then work out how to make money from it, and only then add friction to raise lead quality. In practice he almost always opens a new market with a free or heavily discounted front end, and treats "premium", "free" and "discount" as three wrappers around the same core Grand Slam Offer. The wrapper changes how attractive the gift looks, not what is inside it. Origin story: after a 2016 conference talk, he started what became Gym Launch by giving himself away free: flying to gyms, spending his own money on their marketing, closing their leads and handing over customers, and earning only from what he sold. He did it for 33 gyms over 18 months and made his first million outside his gyms.

## How to apply it
1. Build the core offer first.
2. Pick a wrapper: [[premium-offers]] (the offer on its own), [[free-offers]], or [[discount-offers]].
3. Why he starts free or discounted: room to be rough while he learns, fast testimonials traded for free work, easier early closes when his own conviction is low, and easy referrals and demand.
4. Once demand flows, monetise it (upsells, later offers), then add friction as volume exceeds capacity.
5. If there are more upsells to stack, keep the front end as cheap as possible to keep leads coming.
6. Not getting enough response? Make the front end more appealing: give people who otherwise wouldn't respond a reason to.
7. ($100M Offers) Picture a continuum from easy-to-sell/hard-to-fulfill (you do everything) to easy-to-fulfill/hard-to-sell (you do little). Start at the generous end, then use the cash to build systems that deliver the same promise more cheaply; often only the "how" changes, not the promise. Only after yeses are reliable do you qualify harder, raise price, or include less.

## When to reach for it
New market or new acquisition channel; cold audiences that need a "what's in it for me"; weak response to a straight offer.

## Mistakes he warns about
- Putting the cart before the horse: trying to monetise before creating demand. Optimizing with no cash flow tells you nothing about what to fix ($100M Offers).
- Staying in over-delivery mode after demand is proven until the team breaks; early Gym Launch capped out at about eight on-site turnarounds a month.
- Stripping qualification steps to chase volume: in his experience lead count rose but money made fell ($100M Offers Lost Chapter).
- Letting a promotion change the core offer instead of enhancing it.
- Not knowing the customer's problem better than they do; all businesses profit from that information edge, by showing the problems ahead and solving them through upsells.

## The math
Nothing sits below $0, but price can rise indefinitely. Early Gym Launch took only a refundable $500 deposit, ran and closed each gym's leads on site for 21 days, and kept the upfront cash: about $100,000/mo solo, later a team of eight. Teaching owners to do it themselves meant perhaps a third of the fee per gym but hundreds of gyms a month. He would rather spend $5,000 to acquire $45,000 than $1,000 to acquire $5,000. Quote: "Those who give the most, get the most." (Bonus Chapters, p. 4)
