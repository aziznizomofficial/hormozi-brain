# Ultra-High-Ticket Price Anchor
slug: price-anchor · category: pricing · aliases: ultra high ticket anchor, mac daddy option, ultra high option, start with the highest price, get the gasp

## What it is
The first number a buyer hears sets the scale for everything after it. Adding an option ten or more times the price of your core offer does two jobs: a small share of buyers take it (a big lift in average customer value), and the core offer suddenly looks affordable, so more people take that too. He learned it from a tailor who showed him a $16,000 suit first; the $2,000 suit that followed felt like relief, even though he had planned to spend $500.

## How to apply it
1. Design the most over-the-top version of what you sell: more access, more done-for-you, more speed.
2. Price it at a level where you would be genuinely happy to deliver it, at least 10x the core offer.
3. Present it first. If it is truly the best option, the buyers who can afford it should hear about it.
4. If they balk, step down to the core offer, which now looks modest.
5. The same principle applies to payment terms: quote the full undiscounted total first, then present savings for prepaying ([[billing-frequency]]).

## When to reach for it
- One main offer plus a downsell, and no premium option.
- Buyers push back on your core price with nothing to compare it to.
- You suspect a segment of customers would pay far more (demand is roughly 80/20).

## Mistakes he warns about
- Listing something you are not actually willing to fulfill.
- Pricing it so low that a sale stresses you; keep raising the price until a purchase makes you smile.
- Quoting the cheaper number first; it becomes the anchor.

## The math
Before: $500 core taken by 80% and $200 downsell by 20% = $400 + $40 = $440 average value per customer. After adding a $5,000 option taken by 10% (core drops to 70%): $500 + $350 + $40 = $890, roughly double. He notes a friend added a premium tier and it became nearly all he sold, worth millions a year.
